Forward transport: one year in the real ocean executed

481 hypothetical floating objects, released on 8 March 2014 and carried for 365 days by the ocean that actually existed. Daily GLORYS12V1 surface currents at 0.494 m depth, plus 1.2% of the monthly-mean NCEP wind. No object was steered toward any discovery, any arc, or any destination. Run 13 October 2026 against the Copernicus originals; code and full reports in MH370-Ocean-GLORYS-Data at commit 7d29f63.

Superseded in part, 14 October 2026. This page reports the first year. The same 481 objects have since been carried four and a half years on a wider domain, and the westward transport this page calls rare turns out to begin in the second year: 233 of 481 reach 80°E and 168 reach 50°E, against 13 and 1 here. The limitation was the observation window, not the domain. Everything below remains correct for the year it describes — see Four years of transport.
What this page is, in one sentence. We dropped imaginary bits of floating wreckage into a reconstruction of the Indian Ocean as it was in 2014–15, let the water and the wind push them around for a year, and recorded where the water took them. It is not a probability map, not a search recommendation, and not a statement about where the aircraft came down. It is a description of ocean transport.

What the words mean, physically

Every number below is a statement about water moving, not about computation. Before the results, what each term stands for in the sea:

TermWhat it is, physically
ParticleA hypothetical floating object with no size, no sail area and no shape — a marker for a parcel of surface water plus a small wind push. Real debris has a hull, a waterline and a drag signature; this does not. It is the simplest possible floating thing.
ReleaseThe place and moment we put one in the water: eight latitudes from 33°S to 45°S, five longitudes from 85°E to 98°E, twelve objects scattered within about 5 km of each cell centre, all at midnight UTC on 8 March 2014. These positions are arbitrary sampling of the ocean, not candidate crash sites.
1.2% wind slipA partly submerged object sits in two fluids. The water carries it; the air above pushes the part that sticks out. The accepted shorthand from professional MH370 drift work is that the net effect is roughly 1.2% of the wind speed, added to the current. In a 10 m/s wind that is 12 cm/s — comparable to the currents themselves, which is why it matters.
TortuosityDistance actually travelled through the water, divided by straight-line distance from start to finish. It measures how much the ocean stirred an object versus how far it carried it. A value of 1 is a straight drift. A value of 5 means the object sailed five kilometres of ocean for every kilometre of progress — it spent the year going round in eddies.
Gate crossingThe moment an object drifted past a chosen line of longitude. 80°E, 70°E, 60°E, 50°E head toward Africa, Réunion and Madagascar; 100°E, 105°E, 110°E head toward Australia. Crossing a meridian is a fact about where the water went. It is not an arrival, a landfall or a recovery.
Domain exitThe object reached the edge of the ocean we have data for and we stopped watching. In survival-analysis language it is censored — the object did not finish, it left the experiment. It is emphatically not a beaching, and an object that exits westward has not reached Africa.
Mask exitThe object entered a cell the ocean model marks as land or as having no valid velocity. Without a coastline product we cannot tell a genuine coast contact from a data hole, so these are reported separately and never called a beaching.

One year of one ocean. This is a single realisation: the weather and circulation that happened, once. There is no ensemble over the uncertainty in the current field itself, because only one reconstruction exists. Nothing here is a frequency, a likelihood or a probability, and nothing here should be multiplied by anything.

Eastward transport is the ocean's default here, and it is quick. Westward transport is rare and late, and it does not come back: no object that crossed a western gate ever re-crossed it, against 86 objects that oscillated back east across 100°E. What westward motion exists is a one-way departure from the release region, not part of a swing.

The cartwheel in year one superseded

The proposition was that material released around 35.5–38°S would be carried south and east, spend months circulating in the eddy field, and then turn and run west toward the western Indian Ocean — a long loop, a cartwheel. 240 objects were released in that band.

The correction in one picture. Left: where the 57 south-then-west objects are, with the start of each westward run circled. Right: the same objects as longitude against time. The median object travels east until about day 265, turns west near day 284, and is still moving west when the year ends — which is why the claim that the motion reverses was withdrawn.

Four of the 240 crossed 80°E within this year. Asked chronologically rather than at the endpoint, the motion itself — go south, then run west — occurs 57 times in 240, beginning at a median of day 284. Over four and a half years that band reaches 80°E at rates between 23% and 85% depending on release latitude.

What did happen is the part that is easy to mistake for the idea working. These objects turn constantly. 155 of 240 made a substantial eastward excursion and then came back west by at least two degrees at some point in the year. Median tortuosity in the band is 3.01, reaching 19.8 at the extreme — a typical object travelled about 6,100 km of ocean to end up about 2,000 km from where it started.

That is an eddy field. Mesoscale eddies — rotating parcels of water tens to hundreds of kilometres across — spin an object through long, looping excursions while the mean flow underneath carries it steadily east through this first year. The loops are real and large, and within the year they do not add up to net westward transport. Two claims made here earlier have been withdrawn: that the motion “occurred zero times” (an endpoint flag was read as a chronological test — it occurs 57 times in 240), and that the westward motion “reverses and the eastward mean flow reasserts itself” (it does not reverse; the year ends on it, and the four-year run shows it continuing). The southward excursion is real too, and small: typical objects in this band got about 1–2° further south than their release latitude, mostly within the first three or four weeks, and the furthest south anything in the band reached all year was 41.35°S.

Three physically distinct quantities change together, monotonically, with no reversal across eight latitudes. Northern water stirs and holds; southern water runs. At 33°S an object travels nearly five kilometres for every kilometre of progress — it is sitting in the eddy-rich subtropical interior. At 45°S tortuosity is 1.23, which is close to a straight line: that is the northern flank of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, a broad eastward conveyor with comparatively little to stir an object sideways. The same gradient that suppresses looping suppresses any chance of turning round.

This is a gradient, not a barrier. The 0% at 36.5°S sits between a 5.0% and a 1.7%, and each band holds only 60 objects. Nothing here identifies a boundary, a separatrix or an “event horizon” in the water, and the release latitudes were arbitrary sampling rather than a designed boundary experiment. Read the table as a smooth change in regime with latitude.

How the thirteen got west: around the gyre, the other way

All thirteen western crossers took the same route, and it is not the one the cartwheel imagined. They went north first, then west. Their final latitudes run from 10.8°S to 29.3°S — subtropical, well north of every release.

Physically this is the South Indian Ocean subtropical gyre. The gyre turns anticlockwise: eastward along its southern flank, equatorward on the east side, then westward along its northern limb as the South Equatorial Current. An object leaves the eddy field by drifting equatorward out of it, catches that westward limb, and runs. Nine of the thirteen started at 33°S, three in a single cell at 35.5°S/90°E, and one at 37.5°S/94°E.

The cartwheel proposed a southern circuit returning west. The ocean's actual westward pathway in this year goes round the gyre the other way, through the tropics. Four of the 240 band objects found it — by heading toward the equator, not by completing a loop.

Every object that got west of 80°E, with the other 468 in grey. The grey mass stays southeast; the coloured paths climb north into the subtropics before running west along the gyre's northern limb.

None of these is an African arrival. They are objects that crossed a line of longitude in a model. The single object that reached 50°E did so six days before the run ended, and one crossed 80°E on day 363 of 365 — a crossing the experiment barely contains.

The long glide, G0, in the water consistent with the satellite rejection

The G0 conditional water-contact coordinate — 38.70590°S, 90.41482°E, from the frozen C120 flight continuation — was released alongside the grid, using its coordinates only. No score, no χ² and no terminal diagnostic entered this experiment.

It ran straight east. Tortuosity 1.51. Furthest south 40.18°S, on day 34. Past 100°E on day 88. It left the eastern edge of the data at 119.86°E, and over the whole year it never got two degrees west of where it started — its most westerly point all year was 88.75°E.

Stated carefully, because this is where the project is most tempted to over-claim: this is a property of that coordinate's position in the 2014–15 circulation, and it is not evidence about the aircraft. It is independent of the satellite frequency argument that already rejected G0 at 28σ, and it happens to point the same way — an endpoint there would have had difficulty delivering debris westward. But no odds were computed and none can be from a single ocean realisation, so this does not make the rejection stronger in any formal sense. It is one more thing that would have had to go right, and did not.

How much of this is numerics? checked both ways

Halving the integration timestep from six hours to three and re-running the first 182 days gives a clean, two-sided answer.

Individual paths do not converge. Corresponding objects end a median 54 km apart, and in the worst case 1,636 km apart, after half a year. This is chaotic advection: neighbouring parcels of water in a stirring flow separate exponentially, so refining the timestep produces a different realisation of a path rather than a more accurate one. It is consistent with this project's earlier measurements — 864 m at 17 days, a median 850 km at 508 days under a coarser change.

The convergence pair side by side. The row reading "any western gate: 0 against 0" in the table above is not evidence of agreement, because the first 80°E crossing in the full year does not occur until day 194 — after this pair ends. Both runs report zero because neither had got there yet. The honest check is the full year, where the two give 13 against 11 western crossers, within sampling noise.

Both halves of that matter. The second licenses everything on this page; the first forbids anyone — including this project — from saying where any individual object went. No trajectory on these maps is a prediction of a journey. The population behaviour is the result; the lines are illustration.

Where the experiment loses objects. The dense orange line down the right-hand side of each panel is the 119.92°E edge of the data — not a shore. The green arc inshore of it follows the Australian shelf. At the three-hour timestep a single object left through the western edge, at 45.0°E.

The largest caveat: half the experiment hit a wall that is not in the ocean. 235 of 481 objects (49%) ended against the eastern edge of the available data, which stops at 119.92°E because that is where the Copernicus subset was cut. All 91 clean domain exits left eastward; none left west, south or north. By release latitude this runs from 1.7% at 33°S to 100% at 45°S. So the southern bands' 0% westward access is 0% observed before censoring — for those latitudes, the year was substantially an experiment in how fast objects reach a boundary we drew. What they would have done in a wider ocean is unobserved, and no one should read the southern zeros as the ocean's answer until the domain is extended past Australia.

What this does and does not support

  • Supported: in 2014–15, floating material released between 33°S and 45°S in 85–98°E was overwhelmingly carried east, quickly, and westward transport out of that region was rare, late, one-way, and confined to the two northernmost release latitudes.
  • Supported: the stirring regime changes smoothly with latitude — eddy-dominated in the subtropical interior, conveyor-dominated on the circumpolar flank — and the westward route runs through the tropics along the gyre's northern limb.
  • Not supported: any crash location, any search recommendation, any probability over origins. This experiment never saw a satellite measurement.
  • Not supported: a transport barrier, a boundary latitude, or an “event horizon of drift”. The gradient is smooth and the release grid was arbitrary.
  • Not supported: that debris could not have come from the south. One year, one ocean, and the southern bands were removed from the experiment by the data boundary before they had a year to try.
  • Not addressed: discovery-selection bias, which runs the other way and is the reason this experiment was blind. Western debris recoveries are a sample of objects that reached places where people look. Material that stayed at sea, sank, or went east leaves no record. A forward run that never consults the finds is the only way to see the transport without that filter — and what it shows is that the eastern half of the story is unobservable by beachcombing.

Physics frozen throughout: daily GLORYS12V1 surface currents, Atlas NCEP monthly-mean winds, 1.2% effective wind slip, no explicit Stokes term (the slip already stands in for unresolved wave transport, so adding one would double-count), no diffusion, no object-specific leeway, no breakup, and no fitting to any debris discovery. Winds are monthly means, so daily ocean circulation is resolved and daily wind variability is not.

A note on why this ran at all. The two Copernicus files are split by variable — eastward velocity in one, northward velocity in the other, each spanning all 366 days — and the reader required both components in every file, so it could not open them. That was the obstruction, not any network or storage problem.
