Where on arc 7 the debris could have started runs completed 27–28 November 2026

Floating objects released along arc 7 on 8 March 2014 and carried forward by recorded winds and four reconstructions of the ocean currents, then repeated with wave drift computed directly from a wave reanalysis. Counts on this page are model frequencies. They are not probabilities that the aircraft is at a given latitude.

In plain terms. Debris released on arc 7 north of about 33°S reaches Mauritius, Rodrigues, Madagascar and Tanzania far more readily than debris released south of about 36°S, and almost none of it goes to Australia. Debris released south of 36°S puts a fifth to two-fifths of its objects on or beside Australia, where no debris from the aircraft has been found. Both results hold when the wind term is replaced by wave drift computed directly. The drift does not pick one spot: from about 27°S to 33°S the arrivals are much alike. The flaperon found on Réunion does not separate latitudes at all.

How an object is carried. Each object moves with the surface current of the chosen record, plus a term for wind and waves. For debris other than the flaperon that term is 1.2 per cent of the wind speed, the value CSIRO measured for the official search from undrogued drifters and replica debris at sea. The flaperon carries that term plus the extra leeway CSIRO measured on a replica flaperon, directed 16° or 20° to the left of the wind. Winds are monthly means. An object is followed until it reaches a coast, leaves the area the records cover, or the record ends; objects that stop being followed are counted separately and are never counted as failures to arrive.

The four current records. GLORYS12V1, GLORYS2V4, ORAS5 and C-GLORS. The two GLORYS products come from the same institution and count as one line of evidence.

All four records deliver the flaperon to Réunion by day 508, but they disagree about where from. GLORYS12V1 delivers from every stretch, most from north of 30°S. Across the whole study, none of 2,944 matched comparisons of a stretch at 6 hours against the same stretch at 3 hours was separated. Individual objects do end in different places when the step changes, typically a few kilometres apart at a coast and sometimes thousands; the counts per stretch are stable. The first release placed 20 objects in each stretch, which was too few to compare latitudes; that is why the dense release was run.

ERA5's surface wave drift is 1.3 to 1.75 times the wind term and points 10° to 20° away from it, further north and east. This does not show the 1.2 per cent figure to be wrong. ERA5's value is for the surface itself, where wave drift is strongest; an object with any depth in the water feels less, and CSIRO's figure was measured on real objects. The difference in direction is physical: wave drift includes swell arriving from the Southern Ocean, which is not tied to the local wind. The last two columns are sums at a fixed point and are not paths.

The dense release repeated with wave drift. Wave drift replaced the wind term; it was never added to it. Full surface strength is an upper case. The flaperon kept its measured extra leeway.

Arrivals at Mauritius per 2,000 objects. Northern releases outnumber southern ones in every record under every treatment, by about 3 to 12 times with wave drift. With wave drift more objects reach the African side overall and sooner.

  • Limits of the wave runs. The wave record has no data close to land, so an object stops being followed when it nears any coast, including islands on the way. Counts for places beyond the Mascarenes (Madagascar, Tanzania, Mozambique) are therefore incomplete under wave drift and are not reported here. The wave record ends on 31 December 2017 and stops at 32°E.
  • Half strength. A run at half the surface value was also made as a declared lower case. Most of its objects were still afloat when the wave record ended, so its totals are low everywhere and it is not used as a result.
  • The flaperon at Réunion under wave drift still does not separate latitudes: the two GLORYS records then deliver more from the south and the other two more from the north.

What is and is not established

  • Established within these runs. Debris other than the flaperon reaches the western Indian Ocean much more readily from north of about 33°S than from south of about 36°S, in ORAS5, C-GLORS and one of the GLORYS pair. Southern releases deliver a large share of their objects to Australia and northern releases almost none, in all four records. Both hold when wave drift is computed directly, and neither depends on the time step.
  • Not established. A single starting latitude. From about 27°S to 33°S the arrivals are similar and the drift does not choose among them.
  • Not informative. The flaperon at Réunion, for which the four records disagree.
  • What would change this. Current records that carry debris from south of 36°S to the west as readily as from the north; a measured wind and wave response for the individual recovered pieces; dates of coming ashore much earlier than the dates of discovery.
  • Agreement with published work. The University of Western Australia's drift modelling gives 28°S to 33°S.
