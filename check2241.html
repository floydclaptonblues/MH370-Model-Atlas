The 22:41 measurement and the 266 cruise histories

The 16 November cruise catalogue holds 266 model flights that were built to match the satellite timing recorded at 20:41, 21:41 and 00:11 UTC. One more exchange with the satellite was recorded between those times, at 22:41:22 UTC. It was not used to build or select any of the flights. This page reports what happens when each flight’s prediction for that moment is set beside the recorded value. The project calls this a withheld-measurement consistency check with disclosed prior exposure, and says below what that name does and does not claim.

These are counts of related model flights, not probabilities, and nothing here is a location. The 266 flights share their starting arrivals, their fitted measurements and one model. Flights of one incoming speed and cruise level have nearly the same prediction, so they tend to share one outcome and are not independent tests. A model state in the air at 22:41 is not where the aircraft was, and it says nothing about where the flight ended.

Result 18 November 2026

At 22:41:22 UTC the ground station recorded a burst timing offset of 14,540 µs and a burst frequency offset of 204 Hz. The timing offset measures the time the signal took to travel from the aircraft to the satellite and on to the ground, and so fixes the distance between aircraft and satellite at that moment: one microsecond corresponds to about 150 m. The criterion is the one that accepted these flights at the fitted times: the recorded timing minus the predicted timing must be within ±43 µs, about 6.4 km of that distance. It was fixed in writing before any prediction for 22:41 existed.

The edge of the band passes through one group of flights, and a few microseconds separate the two outcomes within it. The outcomes themselves are numerically firm: computed at half the integration step, every prediction moves by at most 0.00033 µs, and no outcome changes.

Outside the band does not mean shown to be impossible. That firmness belongs to the arithmetic. The recorded timing is not accurate to a few microseconds, and neither are the model’s inputs: the satellite’s position, the calibration, the winds, the stored commands. A flight 0.03 µs outside the band and one 0.10 µs inside it are not distinguished by this measurement in any physical sense. What can be said is that 51 flights do not meet the declared criterion at 22:41 and 215 do. The 51 are not negated, and the 215 are not confirmed. The ground-station log itself gives the scale: it reports timing in steps of 20 µs, and one burst scatters by 29 to 40 µs (next section).

Two further thresholds were named beforehand for description only: 172 of 266 flights are within ±29 µs and all 266 within ±129 µs. They do not alter the outcome at ±43 µs. A smaller residual does not rank one flight above another.

What the ground-station log says about the scale of the measurement added 18 November 2026

After the comparison was complete, the full ground-station log for the aircraft (7,196 signalling units, 7–8 March 2014) was set beside the project’s inputs and results. Nothing was recomputed: this is arithmetic on recorded values and on the residuals above.

The inputs are the log’s. All 19 events of the project’s event table are in the log on the stated channel with the same timing and frequency. The 22:41 value used above is the log’s own, with no correction.

The log reports timing in steps of 20 µs. Every one of its 816 timing offsets on the channel used for these exchanges is a whole multiple of 20 µs, about 3 km of distance between aircraft and satellite. A reported 14,540 µs stands for a range of true values 20 µs wide.

One burst scatters by 29 to 40 µs. From 16:00:13 to 16:11:13 UTC the aircraft was at Kuala Lumpur before departure, inside the half hour the investigation used to calibrate the timing. It is taken to be standing still then: the log holds no position, but its timing drifts slowly and steadily in those minutes, and its frequency offset does not jump until the take-off half an hour later. The 48 bursts sent in those minutes are logged between 14,740 and 14,900 µs. Four ways of estimating the standard deviation of a single burst from the log give 29 to 40 µs. Each hourly exchange in flight is one burst. On that scale the ±43 µs band is 1.1 to 1.5 standard deviations wide on each side.

Within half a reporting step either way, the number of flights inside the band runs from 172 to 266. All 51 flights outside the band are outside it by less than half a reporting step, and by about 0.14 of the scatter of one burst at most.

The model evaluates the exchange at 22:41:22.000; the log stamps the burst at 22:41:21.906, 0.094 s earlier. The recorded timing was then rising by about half a microsecond each second, so that interval corresponds to 0.05 to 0.06 µs: more than the 0.03 µs by which the nearest flight is outside the band. That holds for 1 flight; the others are outside by more. No prediction was recomputed at the logged time.

What this changes. No outcome above is altered: the outcomes are the declared criterion applied as fixed beforehand. What the log adds is the scale against which to read them. The 51 flights lie inside the reporting step of the measurement and far inside its scatter, so they remain possible; and the flights at the edge of the band at the fitted times were accepted with the same margin by which these 51 are not. The scatter was measured before departure, over eleven minutes, on the assumption that the aircraft was not moving, and may differ in flight. The investigation’s own calibration over that half hour reports a largest error of 59 µs among seventeen measurements, which is in keeping with it. Widening the band now, with the result in view, would be a criterion chosen after the fact and was not done.

Each of the 90 flights has 138 sampled responses, and whether a response is compatible with the final transmissions is what the terminal study computed; those classifications stand exactly as published, with their one permanent provenance exception. A flight that meets 22:41 does not make its responses meet 00:19.

How the check was run

  1. The method was written and fixed first. The population (all 266 flights), the recorded event and its fields, the sign of the residual, the criterion and the wording of each possible outcome were set down before any prediction for 22:41 was formed. The program was reviewed, and faults found in review were repaired, before it was allowed to run.
  2. A gate on eleven flights. One flight of each combination was flown again from its stored commands. Each reproduced its catalogue record exactly, and the routine that forms a prediction gave the catalogue’s own stored values at 21:41 and 00:11. Nothing was evaluated at 22:41. The gate was run twice, once for each of two revisions of the program: a fault in its record-keeping was repaired between them, and a gate qualifies only the program it ran.
  3. Predictions for all 266 flights. Every flight was flown again and reproduced its record. One prediction of timing and frequency at 22:41:22 was then formed at the catalogue’s integration step and one at half that step: 532 evaluations in all, none repeated. The two steps agree to within 0.00033 µs and 0.0000042 Hz. The predictions were fixed, with a hash, before the recorded value was selected.
  4. The comparison. Only then was the recorded value selected and subtracted. The comparison was made by the project’s independent reviewer (Codex, an AI model that also reviewed each earlier step), with the program fixed in step 1, and again with a second program written separately from the method in 50-digit decimal arithmetic. The two agree on all 266 outcomes. The first program, run again on the computing machine, gave the same files byte for byte.

The predicted timings span 60.5 µs across the 266 flights (14,526.7 to 14,587.2 µs), less than the 86 µs width of the band. That was known from the predictions alone, before the comparison: the check could at most separate some flights from others, and most of the spread, 44.4 µs, lies between combinations and not within them.

What “withheld” means here, and the limits

  • Withheld from the fit, not unknown. The 22:41 timing entered no search, adjustment or selection that produced these flights, and the cruise run’s guard recorded no request for a prediction there. But the value is public and is shown elsewhere in this Atlas; earlier experiments of this project formed predictions at 22:41 and compared other flights with it; the people and models who chose this catalogue’s speeds, levels and calibration worked after those comparisons existed; and the flight model loads the whole recorded event table, 22:41 included, whenever it starts. The runs of this check that did so are logged. It is not a test against a measurement nobody had seen, and that the catalogue’s design was uninfluenced by earlier 22:41 results cannot be shown.
  • An interpolation along a fitted leg. Each flight already matches the timing at 21:41 and at 00:11. Its last commanded turn starts between 20:56 and 21:26; after it the flight holds its commanded Mach number and pressure level with no further commanded turn. Held Mach is not constant ground speed, since temperature and wind change along the leg, but a prediction partway along a leg that is matched at both ends is constrained by both. The check mainly tests whether the commanded speed and level, the weather fields and the satellite model carry a flight correctly across that leg. Agreement at 22:41 is weaker evidence than agreement at a time the flights were not built around.
  • One model, fixed inputs. One satellite ephemeris and calibration, one wind and temperature field, the stored commands. No uncertainty is attached to a prediction. The spread across flights is a spread across accepted histories, not an error bar. The sensitivity to the satellite inputs identified in the separate satellite audit remains open.
  • Exact reproduction is local. Every flight reproduced its record to the last digit on the machine and numerical libraries that built the catalogue. That is not a claim about another machine.
  • Conditional states. At 22:41:22 the 266 model flights are in the air between 20.5 to 24.5°S and 91.3 to 94.7°E. Those are states of model flights at one moment. They are not an estimate of the aircraft’s position and not a search area.
  • Nothing about the end of the flight. The check concerns the cruise. It does not test the final transmissions, the descent or an impact position.
